# Education levels and audiences

Read this when the education level or audience affects the writing. Precedence: the user's explicit instruction, then the assignment rubric or venue rules, then genre conventions, then this profile. If no level is given and it matters, assume undergraduate. Name that assumption only in a Case C verification report, not in the writing.

Levels differ in what is claimed, what counts as evidence, what is critiqued and how uncertainty is handled. They do not differ by vocabulary difficulty. Never make writing "higher level" by lengthening sentences, adding nominalisations or using rarer words.

These profiles interpret public qualification descriptors in writing terms: US Common Core grades 11-12 writing standards; UK QAA FHEQ levels 6-8 (2024); European Qualifications Framework levels 4-8. They also draw on technical-writing audience guidance.

| | High school | Undergraduate | Master's / postgraduate | Doctoral | Researcher / educator | Professional / technical |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Claim | A precise claim distinguished from opposing claims | A sustained argument or explanation using the field's established ideas | A critical position on current problems; original application of knowledge | A contribution: what this work adds and where it sits in the field | Synthesis or teaching aimed at a defined community | A decision, procedure or recommendation the reader can act on |
| Evidence | Relevant and sufficient; credible sources | Scholarly reviews and primary sources, used accurately | Evaluates studies and their methods; weighs conflicting findings | The writer's own data and analysis to publication standard; exact statistics | Representative, current literature; precise attribution | Verified specifics (versions, values, steps) |
| Critique | Strengths and limitations of claims and counterclaims | Evaluates arguments, assumptions and incomplete data | Critiques methodologies; proposes hypotheses where appropriate | Addresses threats to validity and alternative explanations; justifies design choices | Represents competing views fairly | Trade-offs and risks relevant to the decision |
| Uncertainty | Acknowledges counterclaims | Hedges in proportion to evidence; notes limits of knowledge | Makes judgements explicit despite incomplete data | Calibrated: effect sizes, intervals, scope conditions | Separates consensus from open questions | States assumptions and known unknowns plainly |
| Terms | Defines subject terms on first use | Uses discipline terms accurately; defines central ones | Uses field terminology without over-explaining basics | Precise sub-field terminology and consistent notation | Matches the audience (peers or learners) | The reader's vocabulary; jargon only when the role needs it |
| Structure | Claim, reasons, evidence, conclusion; transitions that show logic | Paragraphs with distinct functions; one line of argument | Organised by problems or positions, not source by source | Genre conventions; contribution signposted | Purpose-led (review, tutorial, guidance) | Task-first, in the order the reader acts |

## Discipline conventions
Follow the field's habits when they are known:
- Passive voice and past tense in methods sections.
- First person where the field accepts it.
- Present tense for established findings in many sciences.
- Numbered citations in engineering and medicine where the venue uses them.
- Signposted sections in reports.

When unsure, follow the rubric or the user's own text.

## Common mistakes to avoid
- Adding statistics or citations to look advanced without a supplied source.
- Making doctoral text more certain; the expected skill is judgement under incomplete data.
- Simplifying high-school writing into vagueness; precise claims are still required.
- Ignoring a rubric because a profile says something else.
